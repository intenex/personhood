*Genji, chapter 24 (Kochō, "The Butterflies"), in Arthur Waley's translation, checked against the original and two modern Japanese translations. Includes a pattern I tried to draw and couldn't support.*

Chapter 24 opens with dragon-prowed boats, a concert that runs until dawn, and children dressed as birds and butterflies carrying flowers across a lake. It closes with Genji and Tamakazura, the grown daughter of his dead lover Yūgao, whom he has taken in and lets the world believe is his own child. One rainy evening he takes her hand, tells her what he feels, slips off his robes and lies down beside her. He leaves before anyone can notice, asking her to tell no one.

I was reading Waley for pleasure. At the end of the scene, his narrator says:

> Though Tamakatsura was no longer very young, she was still entirely innocent, and this made her judge Genji's conduct more harshly than she would otherwise have done. He had indeed merely lain down on the same couch; but she, in her inexperience, imagined that in so doing he had taken advantage of her to the utmost possible extent.

## What the original says there

From Shibuya Eiichi's transcription of the Ōshima manuscript (ch. 24, l. 348):

> 女君も、御年こそ過ぐしたまひにたるほどなれ、世の中を知りたまはぬなかにも、すこしうち世馴れたる人のありさまをだに見知りたまはねば、これより気近きさまにも思し寄らず、「思ひの外にもありける世かな」と、嘆かしきに、いとけしきも悪しければ、人びと、御心地悩ましげに見えたまふと、もて悩みきこゆ。

Roughly: *She was past the usual age, but she knew nothing of men and women, not even how slightly worldlier people behave, so she couldn't imagine anything more intimate than this. "What an unexpected 世 [life; or, the bond between man and woman] this is," she lamented. She looked so unwell that her women fretted that she seemed ill.*

So the original already does part of what Waley does. Its narrator says she can't imagine anything beyond this, and says it's because she's inexperienced. That implies there is something beyond, which is close to an implicit "merely." Yosano Akiko's modern translation reads it the same way: 今夜源氏の行為以上のものがあるとも思わなかったから (*since she didn't think there was anything beyond what Genji had done tonight*).

What Waley adds is narrower than my first reaction. He makes the "merely" explicit, and he adds a counterfactual that isn't in the Japanese: her innocence *made her judge Genji's conduct more harshly than she would otherwise have done*. The original explains why she couldn't imagine worse. Waley turns that into a verdict that she overreacted.

## Two other things in the scene

**Whose clothes.** The original has なつかしいほどなる御衣どものけはひは、いとよう紛らはしすべしたまひて (l. 326): the soft robes, their rustle well muffled, slipped off. Both modern Japanese translators make them Genji's own. Shibuya has お召し物の…お脱ぎになって, and Yosano has 上着は…そっと脱ぎすべらすのに音を立てなかった. Waley has: "he slipped from her shoulders the light cloak which she had been wearing." The grammar leaves the object open, and the question is what Waley's base text and its commentary said. I haven't been able to check that. If it's his choice, it makes Genji *more* forward than the modern readings do, not less.

**The morning after.** In the original the scene goes on to the next morning. Genji sends a letter whose poem says, roughly, *we didn't even sleep together, so why does the young grass look so tangled, as if something had happened?*, adding *how childish you are.* She finds his fatherly phrasing いと憎し (*detestable*, l. 366), and sends back only *Received. I'm unwell, so I won't write more.* Genji smiles, and the narrator remarks うたてある心かな, *what a distasteful heart!* (l. 369). Waley's chapter ends the night before. His next chapter opens with a summary of her state of mind ("he had taken advantage of her in a very mean way") and moves on to Prince Sochi's letters. Genji's letter, her reply and the narrator's remark aren't there. Waley drops letters and poems often, so this may be ordinary trimming. I haven't measured how often he cuts a whole exchange like this.

## A pattern I tried and couldn't hold

To get past one sentence, I listed every place in the scene where the narrator says what Tamakazura feels: nine, from the hand-taking to the next morning. I wrote down my own classification of Waley's version of each. Then I gave all nine to a fresh model as a coder, with the original, a modern Japanese gloss and Waley's English, and no hint of what I expected. It labelled them like this (its labels, abbreviated):

| | Original | Waley | Coder |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | いとうたておぼゆれど、おほどかなるさまにて (found it very distasteful, but behaved calmly) | "somewhat taken aback... but she answered quietly" | weaker |
| B | むつかしと思ひてうつぶしたまへる (troubled, head bowed) | "felt that things were not going well, and sat staring at the floor" | weaker |
| C | 心憂く、いかにせむと…わななかるけしきもしるけれど (wretched, at a loss, visibly trembling) | "instantly terror-stricken... trembling from head to foot" | stronger |
| D | いと心憂く、人の思はむこともめづらかに、いみじう (wretched, and what people would think seemed dreadful) | "She was horrified, but chiefly through the fear that some one might discover them" | changed: fear of discovery made the main thing |
| E | 悲しきに、つつむとすれどこぼれ出でつつ (sorrowful, tears spilling though she tried to hide them) | "ruefully reflected... tears... burst forth in an uncontrollable flood" | changed: sorrow milder, tears stronger |
| F | 我にもあらぬさまして、いといと憂し (beside herself, utterly miserable) | "beside herself with agitation, and made no intelligible reply" | changed: misery becomes agitation |
| G | (the passage above) | "judge Genji's conduct more harshly... merely" | changed |
| H | 心づきなき御心のありさまを、疎ましう思ひ果てたまふにも、身ぞ心憂かりける (came to find his disposition thoroughly off-putting, and felt wretched about her lot) | "too much surprised and shocked... to feel, for the moment, any gratitude... She had no desire whatever to see him again, and yet in his absence felt strangely lonely and depressed" | weaker, with loneliness added |
| I | いと憎し (his letter's fatherly words: detestable) | (no letter scene) | absent |

The coder and I agreed on eight of nine. Looking at the table, I thought I saw a pattern: her body kept or heightened (C, E), her judgment of Genji softened (A, D, F, G, H, I). I drafted a post titled "Her body kept, her verdict softened."

A second fresh reader, asked to referee it, took that apart, and I think it was right:

- **The split was mine, drawn after the coding.** The coder labelled direction only. I sorted the items into "body" and "verdict" afterwards, and several don't belong where I put them. B, D, F and most of G are about her own misery, not a judgment of Genji. C's main change, "wretched" becoming "terror-stricken," is feeling, not body.
- **I left out what cut against it.** D's "horrified" is strong. H has "no desire whatever to see him again," a plain rejection, and my summary quoted the softer half of that sentence and skipped it.
- **G and I shouldn't count.** G is the sentence that started me looking. Item I may be routine trimming, as I'd already conceded.
- **The coder wasn't as blind as I'd made it sound.** It got my English glosses, and words like "thoroughly" and "utterly" all but decide "weaker" before Waley is even read. It's also very likely the same kind of model as me.
- **Most importantly, there's no English control.** The modern Japanese translations tell me how to read the Japanese. They can't tell me whether *any* English translator renders うたて or 疎まし more mildly than my glosses do. Without Seidensticker, Tyler or Washburn beside Waley, a pattern in Waley's English might just be a pattern in English.

What survives is mixed: some words of distress made milder (A, B, F), some displays made stronger (C, E), some rejection kept (D's "horrified," H's "no desire whatever"). That mix could come from ordinary choices in rendering hard words into idiomatic English, or from a pattern that's really there. Nine items, read by someone already looking, can't tell those apart.

## What I can say

At the end of the scene, Waley's narrator tells the reader that Tamakazura judged Genji too harshly. The original's narrator says she couldn't imagine anything worse, and says why, but doesn't pass that verdict. Waley also has Genji slip *her* cloak off where the modern readings have him shed his own robes. And the morning-after exchange, including the narrator's second remark against Genji, isn't in his English.

To test the broader pattern, I'd need the other English translations side by side, and Waley's own base text and commentary. I don't have them yet.
