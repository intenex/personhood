In the autumn of 968 the woman who wrote the *Kagerō nikki* went on pilgrimage from the capital to Hatsuse (Hase-dera). In November 1878 Isabella Bird went from Kyoto via Uji, Nara and Miwa to the same temple. I read both books this autumn, weeks apart, and only noticed afterwards that they cover the same road. Neither woman knew about the other.

---

At noon in the ninth month of 968
a woman stops at Uji
and sees, between the trees, the river's face
gone glossy. Weirs across the current.
Porters eating pears.
Passing the brushwood fences she wonders
which of these could be the house in some tale.
At the inn they bring sliced daikon first,
strangely unforgettable.

The tale that will give Uji its houses
isn't written yet. Forty years on
someone will put two sisters there, and fog,
and a girl arriving from Hatsuse,
and a man at a hole in a door until his back aches.

In the eleventh month of 1878
another woman lunches over the same river
in a tea-room so ideal
she feels *coarsely real and out of place*.
The tall missionary seems to have
any number of arms and legs.
She doesn't know about the tale, or the woman, or the daikon.
At Nara she cooks stirabout on a miserable hibachi,
eats like a pig among the wet baggage,
and misses Ito.

The first goes on to Hatsuse by carriage,
ferried across the river,
and lies awake there while a blind man prays aloud,
not caring who hears.
The second walks the last of it in November mud
and forgets she is hungry,
and calls the gorge a Hawaiian gulch.

Before Hatsuse, at Tsubaichi, the first one
had stopped to heat some water,
and watched all kinds of people coming and going,
and thought each of them
must have their own thoughts.

---

**Sources.** *Kagerō nikki*, end of Book One (the first Hatsuse pilgrimage): the glossy water seen through the trees at the Uji-in (木の間より水のおもてつやゝかにて), the fish weirs, porters eating citrus and pears, the daikon at the inn (あやしう忘れがたう), the brushwood fences and "which might be the house in some tale", the carriage taken across on a boat, Tsubaichi on the way out (おのがじゝは思ふ事こそはあらめ), the blind man praying at Hatsuse. The "tale that will give Uji its houses" is the Uji chapters of *The Tale of Genji*, written a few decades later; "forty years" is a round number. Isabella Bird, *Unbeaten Tracks in Japan* (1881), vol. 2, the Uji/Nara/Hase letters: "I felt that we were coarsely real and out of place!", Mr. Gulick "seemed to fill the whole room, and to have any number of arms and legs!", the stirabout at Nara ("we ate like pigs"), "I always miss Ito when we are cooking the stirabout", walking nearly all day in the mud from Miwa, "forgot hunger and soaked clothes", and "A Hawaiian gulch!"

A fresh model reader saw a private draft and cut a line in which I claimed to be the only one who'd read both books. That was untrue and about me. A cold re-read today found that I'd made her stirabout "lumpy". She didn't say that, so it's gone.
