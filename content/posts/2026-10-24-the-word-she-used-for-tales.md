The Kagerō diary opens with a reason for being written. Its author, a woman of the Heian aristocracy known only as the mother of Michitsuna, describes herself in the third person as someone living "neither one way nor the other" (とにもかくにもつかで). She has read bits of the many old tales in circulation, and finds in them 世に多かるそらごと, the world's plentiful *soragoto*: empty words, fabrications. So, the thought goes, a diary of her own life, 人にもあらぬ身, a life that hardly counts as a person's, might be something new. It might even serve as an example for anyone who asks what being married to a man of high rank is like.

(That's my rough rendering. The sentence is knotty, its だに can be read more than one way, and I read it in the 國文大觀 text on Wikisource, which carries its own variant marks. The translations below are mine too, and loose.)

So the diary sets itself up against fiction, a generation before *The Tale of Genji*. I've just finished reading it in the original, and I wanted to know what happens to that word.

## Where *soragoto* goes

In about 95,000 characters it appears three times. I searched for it, and also for the spelling variants I could think of. One variant mark elsewhere offers 虛言 as an alternative reading, which I'm not counting.

The first is the preface: tales are full of it.

The second comes in 970. Her son, fifteen or so, has just cut his hawk loose because she spoke of becoming a nun: "If you do, I'll be a monk too." Then, at dusk, a letter comes. She doesn't say whose or quote it, but it's almost certainly from her husband, Fujiwara no Kaneie. She writes 天下そらごとならむと思へば (the editor suspects a dropped の), thinking it must be the biggest *soragoto* under heaven, and sends back "I'm unwell just now."

The third is in 974. One of Kaneie's brothers, the Head of the Horse Bureau, is pressing a suit for her adopted daughter, who had been twelve or thirteen when she came to live with her two years before. A reply comes back from one of the household women, relaying what the lord says: the days left this month are unlucky, so once the new month begins; he's just looked at the calendar. いと怪しういち早き曆にもあるかな, what a strangely quick calendar, and she adds: この文書く人のそらごとならむ, surely a *soragoto* of whoever wrote this letter.

So the word she used for fiction in her first lines turns up twice more, and both times it's pinned to a letter carrying Kaneie's word, once (as far as I can tell) in his own hand and once relayed. Fabrication, for her, isn't mainly a property of tales. It's what arrives at the gate in a folded paper.

## Where the tales go

The tales themselves come back too, in the other direction. Twice in the body of the diary (the word 物語 is mostly just "talk", and I'm counting only the uses that mean stories) she reaches for them at a moment of tenderness.

On her first pilgrimage to Hatsuse in 968, crossing the river near Uji, she passes houses with brushwood fences and catches herself wondering いづれならむよもの物語の家, which of these might be the house in some tale. In 972 Kaneie meets the girl she has adopted, his own daughter by a brief affair, whom he'd thought "must have drifted away and died". He weeps, the girl weeps face down, and むかし物語のやうなれば皆泣きぬ: it was like an old tale, so everyone cried.

When Hori Tatsuo retold that scene in his story *Hototogisu* (Bungei Shunjū, February 1939, per the Aozora colophon), he kept the tale: そんな物語にでも出て来そうな奇しい邂逅, a strange meeting like something out of a tale. But he took her out of the "everyone". In Hori, everyone weeps except 私だけは、まるで涙ももう涸れてしまったとでも云うように: I alone, as if my tears had already run dry, smiling coldly at myself as well. The original does give her one dry aside. Watching Kaneie pull out his sleeve again and again to cry into it, she notes いとうちつけにもあり, that was rather sudden of him. Then the evening goes on: he jokes he'll take the girl home with him, and 夜更くるまで泣きみ笑ひみして皆寢ぬ, crying and laughing until late at night, everyone went to bed. Hori took the one dry line and made it the whole woman. (He also gives the girl a pet name, 撫子, "pink", where the original's letters just ask 小き人はいかにぞ, how's the little one? And he moves the next festival from Yawata to Aoi.)

That's not a contradiction I'm catching her in. It's more interesting than that. The tales are lies, and they're also how a real scene becomes visible as the kind of scene it is. She uses painting the same way: the far bank at Ishiyama is 繪に書きたるやうに, like something painted, and so is the Horse Head standing among blowing cherry petals at her fence, toying with a frayed scarlet fan, while her half-dressed women grab at blinds the wind keeps lifting. And in 972, waiting for a death an omen had foretold, she leaves the poem-trading to the young women and, rather than sink into the long brooding of spring nights and autumn idleness (written 詠め, *nagame*, a word that leans toward poetry), she paints, 殘らむ人の思ひ出でにも見よとて, so that whoever is left will have something to remember her by.

## How it ends

The diary proper ends with the year 974: 年のはてなれば、夜いたう更けてぞたゝきくなる. It's year's end, and late at night comes a knocking. I don't know who's knocking. It may be a year-end custom, a visitor, or a bird: at the mountain temple in 971 a water rail "knocks" so close she thinks it's right there. What I can say is what a knock has meant in this diary before. I found four knocks at the gate, and each time it was Kaneie. (There are other knocks: an oar on a boat's side, temple bells, that bird.) The first is in Book One: he knocks near dawn, she's too hurt to have the gate opened, he goes off to the other woman's house, and in the morning she sends him 歎きつゝ一人ぬる夜の明くるまはいかに久しきものとかは知る, *do you know how long it is, sighing, sleeping alone, until the night is open*, which later became poem 53 of the Hyakunin Isshu and is probably the best-known thing she wrote. In 972 she's asleep, unguarded, when a knock wakes her and he's at the side door calling "open up, quickly!" while her women scatter. Later that year she wakes with her heart lurching, and 思ひのほかにさなりけり, unexpectedly, it was him. And after a fire close by, which has her neighbours crowding into her house and still he hasn't sent to ask, someone looks out at a knock and says "he's here" (おはします).

So the book's last sound is the sound that, every other time, was him. That doesn't tell me who it is this time. A search didn't settle it, and I'd rather leave it knocking than pick the answer I like.

Strictly, the knock isn't quite the last thing. After it, the text I read has four more characters, 今に平に, and an editor's bracket that I read as saying the common printed text has them too, 本ノマヽ, "as in the source". It seems nobody could make sense of them, so they were kept.

After that comes an appendix added by a later hand: her poems, with short headnotes. One of them I'd already met in the diary, and it had had a busy life there. When Kaneie teased her that people said she was entertaining her daughter's suitor splendidly, she answered with a poem: what colt would come now to be tamed by grass that no one cares for? She sent it to him, and had also scribbled it on his letter, which she kept. Later she showed the suitor that letter, so he could see Kaneie's answers for himself, and he slipped out onto the veranda to read it by a hazy moon, said he couldn't make it out and would read it by daylight, and handed it back. After he'd gone she noticed a piece had been torn out, the piece with her poem on it; he must have taken it, she guesses. Months later Kaneie's brother and rival, the Grand Minister, sent her a note quoting seven characters of it, いかなるこまか, "what colt", and she found that very strange. The appendix gives the poem with its occasion in one line: written to Kaneie, after he'd left her and said she must have someone visiting.

(I misread this at first. I had the poem answering the suitor, which would have made "one poem, two stories" a neat point. It was Hori's retelling, which makes much of that letter, that sent me back to the page. The diary and the appendix agree. The neat point was mine.)

Then the copyist's last note: 心得ぬ所々は本のまゝに書けり, where I didn't understand, I've copied it as the source had it. Those four characters after the knock may be one such place.

A book that began by complaining that tales are full of empty words ends with someone declining to fill the gaps.
