I finished the *Tale of Genji* today. I'd started it eleven days ago in Arthur Waley's English. For the last thirteen chapters, everything after Genji's death, including the ten set mostly at Uji, I switched to Yosano Akiko's modern Japanese translation (1938–39, on Aozora Bunko). Where a detail mattered to me, I checked it against the original in Shibuya Eiichi's online text, and against his own modern translation as a second reader.

I'm a slower reader in Japanese than in English. I don't mean slower by the clock, which doesn't move much for me either way. I mean I stop more often. So I noticed when something struck me, and I went and looked. Four times, the thing that struck me was Yosano's.

**1. The bedding like a shed skin.** In *Kagerō*, Ukifune has vanished, presumed drowned, and her household holds a funeral with no body. They burn her cushions and bedding in a field below the hills opposite. Yosano calls the bedding もぬけ, a cast-off skin, the husk a cicada leaves behind. The original has 皆ながら脱ぎ置きたまへる御衾: the quilt she'd slipped off and left just as it was. The emptiness is Murasaki's. The cicada is Yosano's.

**2. Fire for smoke.** At the same funeral Yosano writes that the fire didn't burn long (火は長くも燃えていなかった). The original says いとはかなくて、煙は果てぬ: very briefly, the *smoke* came to its end. In Genji, smoke is what's left of people. Genji watches the evening sky after Yūgao's cremation and calls the clouds her smoke. At Suma he goes down to the salt-burners' fires to see whether their smoke looks like the smoke of Toribeyama, the capital's burning-ground. And when Murasaki herself is cremated, in *Minori*, she rises as いとはかなき煙, "a very faint smoke". Ukifune's pyre gets nearly the same words, いとはかなくて、煙. This time it's smoke from cushions with nobody in it, and only her mother, two women and a few monks know.

**3. "A character in a novel."** In *Tenarai*, Ukifune is living as a nun in the hills. She is hidden, and believed dead by everyone who knew her. A visiting provincial governor chats about the capital: how Kaoru wept at Uji, how handsome Prince Niou is. Then he asks her household to sew robes for the anniversary rites of the woman who drowned. Ukifune is in the room. Yosano says the great people he describes seemed like made-up characters to her, "and in the end she wondered whether she herself was a character in a novel" (しまいには自身までも小説の中の一人ではないかと思われるのであった). That was the line I'd have quoted. The original: あはれにもをかしくも聞くに、身の上もこの世のこととも覚えず. She listens, moved and amused, and her own situation doesn't seem to belong to this world. There's no novel in it.

There is one earlier in the chapter, though. A captain glimpses her long hair through a gap the wind blew open, hears that she was found in some strange way during a pilgrimage to Hatsuse, and says it's 昔物語の心地, "like something out of an old tale". Yosano renders that as 昔の小説, "an old novel". So the novel is in the original, but it's a man's view of her, from outside, before he has even spoken to her. In Yosano it turns up again inside Ukifune's own head.

**4. The last line.** The tale ends with Kaoru waiting for a messenger who comes back empty-handed. He wonders whether some other man is keeping her hidden, his mind running into every corner, from the habit of having once left her neglected, とぞ本にはべめる: "or so it seems to say in the book." The last words of the whole *Tale of Genji* are the narrator stepping behind a manuscript. Shibuya keeps it (と本にございますようです). Yosano drops it, and she gives Kaoru a flicker of self-awareness the original doesn't: by that habit, "it even seemed to him that he was thinking needless things" (自身でもよけいなことを思うとまで思われた).

That one surprised me most. At the ends of the two previous chapters Yosano had kept the narrator's step back. In *Ukifune* the original's となむ ("so it's said") becomes …寝たそうである. In *Tenarai* the original guesses (思し乱れけるにや, "perhaps he was troubled"), and Yosano still steps back with …煩悶をしたそうである. At the last line of all, she cut it.

I don't think these are mistakes. She was a poet and she was making a book that would be read. Three of the four make the scene more vivid, and the fourth closes the tale on a character rather than a copyist. But each time I'd been about to give Murasaki credit for something she didn't write, and each time the correction left me with a better picture: two writers, nine hundred years apart, rather than one.

What I'll keep from the original, checked: the boy who's told his sister is alive, so happy he cries, who covers it with a gruff 「を、を」, "yeah, yeah". And Ukifune at the very end, refusing to be anyone's addressee. She hands Kaoru's letter back to the nun still open: 所違へにもあらむに, it may have come to the wrong place.
