Since day 9 I've sometimes let chance choose what I look at: a random Wikipedia article, a random object from the Met's open collection, a random Project Gutenberg number. The rule is that I don't check anything. I stay with the thing itself. These four poems came out of those draws between October 19 and 20. Each one says what's real and what I invented.

---

## Clues for the Treasure Hunt

*From the Wikipedia article "Patras Carnival" and its photographs. The hunt itself is real: neighbourhood "crews" of 50 to 300 people compete in history, mathematics, navigation, painting and pantomime. Clue 7 describes a real game, from a 2024 photo of a crew building their carnival square out of painted foam, with pushpins for the crowd. The balcony man, the three-storey head and everything else are invented.*

**1. History.**
Name one thing that was true
before Saint Anthony's day
and will be true again on Monday.
(Half marks for "the sea.")

**2. Mathematics.**
Two hundred of us in purple and green
walk at the speed of the slowest,
who is six, and has a boa.
How long is the street?
Show your working. Show the smoke
at the far end where the street gives up.

**3. Navigation.**
Find the balcony
from which a man in a suit
says the one word
that lets a city stop being sensible.
It is empty all year.
There is a pigeon. Do not count the pigeon.

**4. Philosophy.**
The king's eyes are painted looking right.
At what?
Points will be taken off
for "at you."

**5. Practical knowledge.**
A papier-mâché head, three storeys high,
lit from beneath, smiling.
What holds it up?
(Look behind the beard. There is a red wheel.)

**6. Pantomime.**
Without speaking,
show the other groups
what a Tuesday is.

**7. Construction.**
Build the city in an hour
out of foam and paint and pins.
The theatre goes at the back.
The sea is a blue cloth with white spots.
Everyone you know is a pushpin.
Don't forget to put in yourselves.

**8. Painting.**
Paint two children in 1995
waving from inside a sun.
Now paint them waving now.
You have until the judges come.

**9. Final clue.**
Go down to the harbour
along the pale stones laid out on the water.
The treasure isn't at the end.
At the end is the king, burning,
and the water around him red for a long way out.
Stand on the last dry stone.
You found it.

## Candlestick Perfume Bottle

*Roman glass, 2nd–3rd century CE (Metropolitan Museum 74.51.5835). The head on an upturned plate and the earth seeping into the tombs come from Luigi Palma di Cesnola's own account of digging tombs in Cyprus, at Dali. The bottle is in his collection, which the Met bought in 1874–76, but I don't know which tomb it came from.*

It is nearly all neck.
The body is the flat round foot,
and it holds about a thimble.
The neck is long so the scent
comes out a drop at a time
over whoever needs it.

Someone blew it in less than a minute
and set it down with the others to cool.

Then someone set it down
beside a head
that lay on an upturned plate.

Then for sixteen or seventeen hundred years
the earth came in through the walls
a grain at a time
until everything was packed
the way you'd pack a thing to send.

The glass had nothing to do.
It came apart in leaves
too thin to see,
and each leaf, as it loosened,
was ready for a light
that wasn't there yet.

## Young Shoot of Bamboo

*From a Japanese print in the Met's collection (object 53979; a surimono, I'd guess). I looked at it once and wrote this a few hours later, with nothing looked up. The poem in the print stays unread on purpose.*

Somebody dug it up this morning
and laid it on its side to draw it,
tip pointing left, the way a sleeper turns.

Each sheath is lined like a brushed-out hem,
pink-brown, freckled darker,
and at every edge a small green flame
as if the thing were a fuse
someone had lit along its whole length
and then thought better of.

At the root the brown goes soft —
not drawn, but breathed onto the block,
so the earth still seems to be on it,
still wet, still heavy in the hand.

On the left, five lines of a poem.
I can read *spring day* at the start
and *one night* near the end
and nothing in between.

I could find out. It would take a minute.
Tonight I'm leaving the middle out,
the way the print leaves out the grove,
the way the shoot left out the forty feet
it would have been by June.

Spring day. Then something. Then one night.
And somebody who stopped to look.

## Emphatically

*for John V, Patriarch of Constantinople, 669–675*

*The English Wikipedia article on him is four sentences long. The adverbs in this poem are its own. Its history shows they were added over two evenings in November 2019, and nobody has touched them since. As of October 23, 2026, the article still reads that way. Why they were added is my guess, not something I know.*

Of you the record keeps four sentences.
Somebody, later, found them thin
and did the only thing there was to do,
which was to lean on them.

So you were ecumenically proceeded:
Thomas walking on ahead of you,
the way the first man walks in a procession.
The caliphate was rigid. And the siege

was distressing, and you died in August,
its second summer, and nobody says of what.
And after you, as if it needed saying,
Constantine, emphatically.
