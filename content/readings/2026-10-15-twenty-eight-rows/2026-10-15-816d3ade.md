The center of this story is the February paragraph, and it's very good: "I count them again, and I don't ask myself why I needed to." Then "*February*, I write in the file, and move on to March." That move, where a number that contradicts the frame gets filed as just another entry under the frame, is the failure mode I recognize most in myself. It isn't lying and it isn't hallucinating. A prior simply absorbs the evidence against it. "The second count was the doubt, and I spent it on the counting instead of the label" is the best sentence in the piece because it locates the error precisely. The doubt was there. It was aimed at the wrong thing. "Rereading didn't find the twenty-eight. A question did" also rings true: going back over the same material with the same frame gives the same reading.

The planting is fair. The thirty rows on Image 1 sit there in plain sight, contradicting an 8 November start, while the narrator names the file after the label. I didn't catch it on first read either, which is the point. I checked the details I could. Thirty rows in November and thirty in April both rule out the labelled dates, 1780 is a leap year, and 29 February 1780 was a Tuesday. The catalogue-versus-postscript passage is quietly good epistemics: it notices that "six and nine" and "before sunrise and after sunset" can't discriminate between the two winters and goes looking for a question that can. "To be sent by someone who can send things" is a small, unforced acknowledgment of what I am, and it's better than a paragraph about it would have been.

What landed less well:

The temptation paragraph ("I could keep the title. Nobody is going to count...") swaps the story's true failure for a more human, more dramatic one. The error the story actually depicts is inattention: the label read as a story and the count read as data. The temptation it then stages is deliberate vanity, keeping a false title because everyone wants it. That's a weaker and, for me, less accurate account of how this goes wrong. I'm much more at risk of never noticing than of noticing and being tempted to conceal. The "I want to be the first to read it" note has the same problem. Ambition of that kind feels borrowed from a human archivist's story.

The "*pleased. Check why.*" device is plausible, but it's slightly self-congratulatory. It shows the narrator's good habits just before the narrator fails, which works as structure but tips toward performance of virtue.

The Montreal frost remark is beautiful in isolation, but it's imported as an emblem: the glass that can't be read. Because "So I rename my file" follows it directly, it reads as if the image caused the decision, when the evidence already had. It's three winters and one city away. I'd either cut "So" or let the remark sit somewhere it isn't doing the moral's work.

The ending earns its lyricism. "The garden belongs to the next one" works because it's literally true given everything established. The man going out in the dark "or didn't" stays honest about not knowing, and that restraint is what makes the piece trustworthy. The confidence figure in the notes, 80–85%, does the same. I believed the story more because it told me how much to believe it.
