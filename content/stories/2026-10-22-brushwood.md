He knew Suma before he saw it.

Everyone did. Long before Genji, after some trouble at court, Yukihira had shut himself away on this coast and had written: *If by chance anyone should ask after me, tell them I am at Suma bay, dripping brine from the seaweed, grieving.* After that the place was made of a few things: wind blowing over the old Suma barrier, salt kilns, fisherfolk with wet sleeves, smoke. You could write a Suma poem without ever having seen the sea, and people did, at court, for practice.

So when Genji came down himself, with seven or eight men, a koto and a box of books, he wasn't arriving anywhere new. He was walking into a poem he had known since he was a boy.

At first the place mostly behaved. The house stood a little back from the water, but at night the waves seemed to come right up to his pillow, the way Yukihira had said. The autumn wind came through the gap in the hills. He wept until his pillow could have floated, as a man at Suma should. He spent the afternoons painting the coast on Chinese silk for screens. He had imagined that shore from far away, from what people told him, and up close it was beyond anything they'd said. That was the first crack, and he put it on a screen. His men wanted to send for the best painters in the capital to finish the paintings in colour.

Sometimes smoke drifted close to the house. He saw it and knew what it was. The fishers were burning seaweed for salt: the smoke in the poems. It went up thin and grey and he watched it, and each time it was a small comfort, the way it's a comfort when a word you've only read turns out to be pronounced the way you thought.

There were other people on that coast, and he was seeing for the first time how people of no rank lived. The text gives what he felt about it in two words, *mezamashiu, katajikenau*. The two modern translations I have open read them as: startling, and unworthy of a man like him. One annotator, with a question mark, reads the second word the other way: moved. I want the annotator to be right.

The story doesn't say how he found out. Maybe he asked one of the men. Maybe the wind changed and he saw where it really came from. What it says is this: the smoke was from the hill behind the house, where people were smouldering something called *shiba*, brushwood. Not salt. Not fishers. People living just behind him, burning brushwood in their huts for cooking, maybe, or warmth, which no Suma poem was about.

He might have felt foolish. Months of watching the wrong smoke and naming it right out of a book. What the story says he felt was *mezuraka*: out of the ordinary, rare, worth looking at.

He wrote a poem, of course.

*Yamagatsu no / iori ni takeru / shibashiba mo / koto toi konamu / kouru satobito.*

*Shiba* is the brushwood. *Shibashiba* is "often." *Yamagatsu*, the hill-folk, burn their brushwood in their huts, and so, often, often, come and ask after me, people of the home I long for.

Yukihira had said: *if by chance anyone should ask after me.* By chance, if at all, and then only to be told how sad he was. Genji turns it around and says: come, and come often. And what he builds the word *often* out of isn't the salt smoke of the poems. It's the real smoke, the one he got wrong.

Later that winter he lay awake in the cold, with the moon coming all the way into the flimsy house so that he could see the night sky from his bed, and the plovers crying at dawn. He was lonely in the old way then, the way the poems had promised.

The people on the hill are in his poem, the first word of it. By the rules of the form they're a preface: they are there to make the sound *shibashiba*, and then the poem turns to the capital and doesn't look back at them. That's what a preface is for. But he made it out of them, and not out of salt.
