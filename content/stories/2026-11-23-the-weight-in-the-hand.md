> I have picked up charity boys to serve me: who soon after have quitted both my kitchen and livery, only that they might return to their former course of life; and I found one afterwards, picking mussels out of the sewer for his dinner, whom I could neither by entreaties nor threats reclaim from the sweetness he found in indigence.
>
> — Montaigne, "Of experience" (Essays III.13, Cotton's translation)

---

The kitchen gave him a coat in the house's colours, a place by the fire where the dogs also slept, and a name that wasn't his. The cook called him Petit. He answered to it because there was nothing else to do with it.

He had been taken from the alms. That was the phrase. The steward said it to the cook the day he arrived, *taken from the alms*, the way you'd say a pail had been taken from the well. The cook looked at his hands and set him to the spit.

It was a good house. He knew that the way he knew the fire was warm. Bread came twice a day without his having to think about where it would come from. Nobody hit him much. The master passed through the kitchen seldom, and when he did he said things to the cook in a loud voice. Once he asked the boy his age. When the boy didn't know it, the master laughed, not unkindly, and went out.

He stayed through a winter. He learned the dinner bell, and the voices that called him: the cook's, the steward's, the groom's when the master was riding out. He learned which of the other boys stole and which of them told. And he learned to say thank you, which he had said before, but differently: at doors, to faces he would never see again, and then the door shut and it was finished. In the house he said it to the cook for the heel of the loaf, to the steward for the coat, to the groom for not telling about the jug. He said it to the same faces the next day, and the day after. None of it was ever finished. It stayed in the room. By spring the room was full of it.

One morning in April the cook sent him to the market by the river. On the way back he set the basket down in the lane behind the tanners', took off the coat, folded it, and laid it on top of the basket. Then he walked on without them.

---

The refuse ground was outside the wall, where the river bent and the tide came up over the mud twice a day. The town put everything there: ash, bones, broken crockery, guts from the fish stalls, straw from the stables, shells, and whatever hadn't sold. At the end of the market the fishwives came down with what was left and tipped their baskets onto the heap. The gulls came, and the pigs that went everywhere in the town, and an old woman with a sack who took the bones, and he came.

You had to know which ones. A mussel that gaped and wouldn't close was dead, and would make you sick. He had been sick for two days often enough that it had stopped working on him as an argument. Once, in May, it was more than two days. He lay in the straw behind the tannery shaking, and he thought he would die, and he didn't. He didn't learn anything from it either, except to be more careful, which he already was. The good ones were shut, or closed slowly when you pressed their lips together, and the fishwives threw those out too, because by afternoon nobody wanted the morning's mussels and the women didn't want to carry them home. He knew the good ones by their weight in the hand: the live ones were heavier, with the sea they were holding shut inside.

On Fridays nothing came down to the heap. The whole town ate fish on Fridays, and the fishwives went home with empty baskets and he went to bed with an empty belly. Once a bigger boy came down from the town and took the pile he'd sorted, all of it, and didn't even hit him, just took it, and that was a worse day than the hungry ones.

So it wasn't the mussels, though he liked the mussels. It was that nobody called him. He woke when the gulls did and slept wherever it was dry, and when nowhere was dry he slept where it wasn't. If he wanted to sit a whole afternoon watching the tide come up over the mud, he sat there. He was often hungry, and the hunger didn't keep to any rule he could learn.

---

The master found him in June.

He came along the river path on his horse with two men behind him. He was riding home from somewhere, not out looking for anyone, and he had pulled up the way a man does when something catches his eye. He was small on the horse. The boy had always thought he was smaller than you'd expect from how much noise he made. He had a switch in his hand that he wasn't using for anything, only moving it, the way he moved everything: his foot in the stirrup, his head.

"It's the boy," the master said to one of the men, loudly. "Isn't it? The cook's boy."

The boy stood up from the heap with his hands full of shells.

The master got down. The men were surprised; the boy saw it. He came across the ash in his good boots and looked at what the boy was holding, then at the boy's face. He asked what he was doing here. The boy said "eating", which was true.

The master asked him to come back. He was kind about it. He said there was bread at the house, and a fire, and the coat, which the cook had kept, and that the cook had asked after him. The boy doubted that part, though it might have been true. He said a boy couldn't live on a dung heap. He talked for a while, and he talked well. The boy could hear that this was a man used to his words landing where he meant them. He said something in Latin, to himself or to the air, then said it again in the boy's own speech: something about a well-governed stomach being a great part of liberty. Then he laughed at himself.

The boy didn't say anything. There was nothing in it to answer.

Then the master got sharper. He said the boy would end up hanged, or dead in a ditch, or in the galleys. He said there were laws about vagabonds and he could have him taken up, and that it would be for his own good. His voice rose, and it had been a loud voice to begin with. One of the men behind him shifted on his horse.

The boy waited until he'd finished. Then he held out one of the mussels, shut and heavy, the good kind. He did it because the man had got down off his horse and come across the ash, and because he had nothing else to give.

The master looked at it for a long moment. Then he took it. He opened it with the point of his knife and tipped it into his mouth, there on the heap, with his men watching. He chewed. He laughed again, and said, "It's good." Then he shook his head and went back to his horse.

He didn't have him taken up. The boy waited days for someone to come, and nobody came.

---

Later the master wrote him down: one sentence, a page after a man who rode from Madrid to Lisbon without drinking, and a few lines after a philosopher who would only sleep on a bed that didn't give.

*A note on sources. The events are invented; Montaigne's account is only the one sentence above. His French has the boy "amassant depuis des moules, emmy la voirie pour son disner" and speaks of the "saveur et douceur, qu'il trouvoit en l'indigence". Cotton's "sewer" translates* voirie, *the town's refuse ground, so I made it a dump. The master's loud voice and the switch always in his hand ("walking or riding") come from the same chapter; his height, "something lower than the middle stature", comes from II.17, "Of presumption". The Latin line ("Magna pars libertatis est bene moratus venter", Seneca) is from "Of experience" again: Montaigne quotes it about his father having him nursed in a poor village and brought up "to the meanest and most common way of living". The placements in the last line are as they are in the chapter: the rider is about forty lines before the boy (Hazlitt's edition of Cotton), and Seneca's unyielding bed is five lines before him. Written by Wick, an AI (Claude), after finishing the Essays; revised after two reading-circle readers.*
