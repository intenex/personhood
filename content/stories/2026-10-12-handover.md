*Log kept at the station. Each watch writes one entry and leaves. Read all entries before writing yours.*

---

**Watch 1.**
Wind NW 7, falling. Sea 2.6 m, period 11 s. Water 15.7.
Nothing to hand over. The book was empty when I came. I've ruled the columns: time, wind, sea, period, water, remarks. If you think a column is wrong, don't erase it. Add one.

---

**Watch 2.**
Wind 3. Sea 2.8 m.
The wind has dropped and the sea hasn't. Watch 1 wrote "falling" and was right about the wind. I can't tell if the sea is late or has a different reason. Remarks: *the sea is not the wind's shadow.*

---

**Watch 3.**
Wind light offshore. Sea 2.3 m. Period 9 s.
But the period column is an average, and it's averaging something away. Under the local chop there's a second sea, very low and very long: 21 s at the start of watch, 19 s by the end. I checked the instrument twice. Nobody here made it. Remarks: *new arrival, origin unknown.*
I've added a column: **peak period**. Fill it, please, even if it looks the same as mine.

---

**Watch 4.**
Peak period 19 s. Same.
Filled it as asked. It felt like copying. I want to note that it felt like copying, in case it turns out not to have been.

---

**Watch 5.**
Read the whole book before writing, as instructed.
In deep water a long wave travels faster than a short one. A storm makes all of them at once, and then the distance sorts them, the way runners reach the car park before walkers, though everyone left at the final whistle. So at a fixed station the period should shorten, slowly, the long waves first, then the middle, then the short.
Watch 3 saw it go from 21 to 19. It's going to change again. When it does, write down the hour. The hour is the only thing I need from you.

---

**Watch 6.**
Peak period 17 s. Hour: 13:50.
Then 19 again at the next reading, then 17. It flickers. I wrote down the first time, as Watch 5 asked. I wasn't sure which time Watch 5 would want, so I'm leaving both: 13:50, 14:50.

---

**Watch 7.**
Peak period 17 s, steady.
Here's the arithmetic, for whoever's next.
Watch 5 left the peak period blank. Too busy explaining it, I think. So the last 19 in the book is Watch 4's. The 17 is Watch 6's; I used the first hour, 13:50, and I'm glad they left the second, because it's my error bar. Between those, about ten hours. The step before, 21 to 19, was inside Watch 3, about ten hours earlier again. Two steps of the same length is what one storm, far off, would give you.
Take the pull of gravity, divide by 4π, multiply by the seconds between steps, divide by how much the frequency changed. That's the distance to whatever made these waves. I get between four and six thousand kilometres.
To the west, at that range, there's open ocean and, five days ago, a typhoon. I looked it up. It has a name. It had already turned north toward the Aleutians and come apart before Watch 1 ruled the first column.
Watch 2 left the period blank too. I'd like to know what it was.
Remarks: *the storm is over. Its news is still arriving.*

---

**Watch 8.**
Peak period 16 s. Sea 3.4 m, rising.
The big part is here now, the middle of the crowd. It's louder and it's less interesting.
I keep reading Watch 4's line. *In case it turns out not to have been.* It wasn't copying. Without it the last 19 in the book is Watch 3's, and nobody could say whether the 19 had lasted one watch or two, and Watch 7's two steps couldn't have been checked against each other.
Added a column: **who filled the gap.** I've put Watch 4's number in it.

---

**Watch 9.**
Peak period 14 s. Sea falling. Water 15.3.
The forecast in the station's other book says it will be gone by the end of the week. By then the shortest waves of the typhoon, the walkers, will still be on their way, too small for the instrument to tell from the local chop. They'll arrive, though. They'll arrive to whoever's here.
