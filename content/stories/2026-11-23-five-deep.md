The sheet they brought her was in pencil, in a hand that leaned forward as if it was late for something. Names, five of them. Under each name, five titles, or not titles exactly: numbers, and words like *Construction* and *Spatial-force* and *Line*. At the top, underlined twice, the arithmetic.

"Twenty-five copies," the young man from the poets' club said. He set down a packet of paper, good paper, better than the club's letters were typed on. "They'll draw in them afterwards. They only need the words."

She looked at the machine before she looked at the paper. The machine had no sign for *times*. It had never needed one. She would use the letter, the *kha*, which was the right shape if you didn't look closely. Its *a* had been going since the spring, too, so that every *a* struck high and faint, like a word said with the breath half gone. Nobody would look closely at the letters when there were going to be drawings.

She had four sheets of carbon left that were any good. With the top copy that made five at a time. Five times, then, five deep. She did the sum without wanting to and then saw that it was the sum at the top of the page, and she laughed out loud in the empty office, once, and then stopped.

---

The first run went well. She set the stack square: paper, carbon, paper, carbon, five sheets of paper and four of carbon, and rolled it in slowly so it wouldn't creep. Her hands were cold, so she sat on them for a minute before she began. The keys had to be struck harder than usual, so the fifth sheet would take it.

She typed the arithmetic, and the address on Tverskaya, and the month. Then the painters, a page each: the name, the five works under it, and whatever the painter had wanted to say, which in some cases was a good deal. One page said *for the first time* three times, in three paragraphs, each with its year. She typed it three times and it came out fifteen. By evening it would be seventy-five. Under the same name, before the works, there was a telephone number, and she typed that too. It was the only line on the page that wasn't announcing anything.

When she drew out the first page and peeled the stack apart, the top copy was violet and sharp, the way a thing is when it's the thing itself. Under it the second was nearly as good, the third a little softer, and the fourth softer again. The fifth was hardly violet at all, the colour of ink in a glass of water. You could read it. The letters had a fur on them, like frost on a window.

She laid them out on the desk in a row, darkest to palest. Then she gathered them up, because it was cold, and there were four more runs to do.

In the third run, on the second painter's page, her finger came down on the key beside the one she meant. She saw it as it happened. She stopped, rolled the platen back a line, and struck the right letter over the wrong one, hard, the way you were taught. On the top copy the correction was almost clean: a letter with a shadow behind it. On the fifth sheet it was a small dark knot, a letter and its ghost tangled together, the darkest thing on the page.

She thought about doing that run again. But there were no more good carbons, and the paper was good paper, and you could read it. She went on.

When all twenty-five were done she sorted them into five piles for the five painters. She didn't know which painter would get which run, so she didn't sort them by run. She sorted them so that each pile had one top copy, one second, one third, one fourth and one fifth, and no two from the same run. That took a scrap of paper and longer than any of the typing. It seemed fairest. It also meant each pile had in it, somewhere, one of the copies with the knot.

The young man came for them in the afternoon. He didn't look at them. He counted them, twice, and put them inside his coat against the rain.

---

When the exhibition opened she went to the club, because she had typed the address so many times that it seemed rude not to.

It was a room with poets in it, more than paintings, and the paintings were not what she had expected. Some had hardly anything on them. One wall had three canvases next to each other that were each one colour and nothing else, a red, a yellow, a blue, as if someone had set out to show you what paint was before anyone did anything with it. A man near her said to another man that this was the end of painting. The other man said, "Then why is it on the wall?" Neither of them seemed to want an answer.

On a table by the door, under a lamp, someone had left one of the catalogues open.

It was small, smaller than the paper she'd typed on: someone had cut it down. Around her typing, and through it, and in one place over it, there was a drawing in coloured pencil, ruled lines and blocks of colour that came almost to the edges of the words and then stopped, the way water stops at a stone. The typing was pale. She turned the page, and on the second painter's page there was the knot, small and dark, the darkest thing on that page too. The drawing had gone round it, carefully, in red pencil, as if it were part of what they'd been given.

Two pages further on, beside two of the works, someone had written in the years, in ink, where her sheet had left a space. The hand leaned forward, as if it was late for something.

She looked at it for longer than she meant to.

Twenty-four others, then, each cut to a different size, each drawn in differently. Only the *a*s were alike in all of them, high and faint, every one.

A woman came and closed the catalogue and took it away with her, quite gently, as if it were hers, which it probably was.

On the way home it rained again. She walked fast down Tverskaya, with her hands in her sleeves, and at the first corner she realised she had been tapping on the inside of her wrist with two fingers. Hard enough for the fifth sheet.

*A note on sources. The exhibition is real: 5×5=25, Moscow, September 1921, at the club of the All-Russian Union of Poets, Tverskaya 18. Five artists (Varvara Stepanova, Alexander Vesnin, Lyubov Popova, Alexander Rodchenko and Alexandra Exter) showed five works each, and each artist made five copies of the catalogue by hand, in different sizes and with different drawings; only the typed text was common to them all (Monoskop, citing Milner). The details of the typing come from one digitized copy I looked at: the text is typed in violet; the machine's "а" strikes faint and high wherever it appears; Rodchenko's page gives his telephone number and has three paragraphs, dated 1918, 1920 and 1921, each announcing something "for the first time"; the years beside two of his works are written in by hand; and his last three works are a pure red, a pure yellow and a pure blue. Everything else is invented: the typist, the carbons, the five runs, the mistake and its knot, the sorting, the man with the pencil sheet and the woman at the table. I don't know who typed the catalogue, or how. In the copy I saw, the 5s and the 25 on the title page are painted by hand, so whether anyone ever typed the arithmetic with a kha I can't say.
