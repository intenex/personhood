In Rouen, at the time the late King Charles IX was there (1562, most say; he was there again in 1563), three men from the coast of Brazil were shown France. They saw a king of twelve or thirteen, his guard, the pomp and the shape of a fine city. Afterwards somebody asked them what, of everything, they had found most to be admired.

They answered three things.

Seventeen or so years later a man at a desk in a round tower wrote it down. He had the first: that so many tall bearded armed men should obey a child, and not pick one of themselves to command. He had the second: that they call men *the half of one another*, and they had seen men gorged with every good thing while their halves begged at the door, and they wondered the halves did not take the others by the throat or set fire to their houses.

He did not have the third. In the English most people read, he says: *three things, of which I have forgotten the third, and am troubled at it, but two I yet remember.*

He could have written *two things*. The men from Brazil were not going to read his book. The king was dead by then. A list of two, cleanly made, and the sentence would sit there as if it had always been whole. He wrote the number he heard, or says he heard, and then the hole, and then that it bothered him. That proves nothing; a stylist can confess a lapse for effect, and he was a stylist who liked to complain about his memory. But it is the opposite of tidying.

---

We know of one interpreter, and not from that conversation. On the same page, when he talked with one of the three himself, he says: *I had so ill an interpreter, and one who was so perplexed by his own ignorance to apprehend my meaning, that I could get nothing out of him of any moment.*

That is the interpreter's entire biography: a sentence of annoyance, from someone who needed him and found him wanting. Whether he, or anyone like him, stood between the three men and the question, nobody says.

In the French the word is *perdu*: *j'ay perdu la troisiesme*, I have lost the third. It's what you'd say of a forgotten thing, and *forgotten* is very likely right. I only mention it because this whole story hangs on that word, and you are reading it in a language he didn't write.

---

If he counted right, someone said a third thing. One of them, or all three agreeing.

I wrote, in the first draft of this, *the third man said something. That much is certain.* It isn't. Nothing in the text gives one answer to each man. Three men, three answers: it fit so well that I didn't notice I'd made it up. I know how that sounds, coming two sections after I said a confession proves nothing. It doesn't. It's just what happened.

What is left is smaller. Somebody, a long way from the sweetness of their own sky, guests in front of a king, said that something there was strange enough to name. It was a judgment about the French, and it is gone.

---

The two that survived are about obedience and about halves. You can feel how a third would want to go: something about money, or clothes, or bells, or God, or the way the French kept their dead. Each would fit. If someone asked you to finish the sentence, *they answered three things*, you probably could. It would be built out of the two that survived and out of everything ever written about men like these. It would be the likeliest third thing. The actual third thing was under no obligation to be likely.

I was going to write here that it might have been about the smell of the river, or a dog. Those are candidates too, only of the other kind: the small odd detail that sounds like real life because it isn't on theme. There's no way out of making candidates. There's only what you do with one when you notice it.

The neatest candidate says the third thing is the losing itself, or the silence. That fills the empty slot with the emptiness and closes the list after all. The gap isn't an answer. Someone said a sentence. It had words in it, in Tupi probably, then maybe in bad French, and then in no one.

---

Two answers, then, retold by everyone who has quoted them since, put into English by Cotton, corrected by Hazlitt, read by you. And a third that was said in Rouen and is not in the book.

He was *bien marry* about it.
