# The degrees of cold

## 1. What is left

All I can read of the winter of 1779–80 at Trois-Rivières is a postscript, not the table it belongs to. The table is in London, on paper, in a bound volume in the Royal Society's archive. The photographs filed under it seem to show the next winter instead. The Society's online catalogue has the archivist's transcription of the postscript:

> The above Observations were taken by Fahrenheit's Thermometer, which was placed in a Garden. The degrees of Cold were marked down at 6 O'Clock in the morning, & at 9 O'Clock in the Evening. The letters a & b, denote the Mercury to be above or below 0.

*The above Observations.* On the pages above it there are numbers. I know their format, because I've worked through four of William Barr's other winters: day, morning, evening, snow, rain, wind, remarks. There are a hundred and sixty-one days, from the 8th of November to the 16th of April. Each cell is a degree, then *a* or *b*, then a small *o*.

That was the winter New York harbour froze, the one the eastern colonies called the Hard Winter. I haven't found a thermometer record from the St Lawrence valley for that winter in the compilations I know (HCLIM, and Victoria Slonosky's reconstructed St Lawrence series, which is empty from 1755 to 1797). His may be the only one, and it's the one I can't see.

## 2. Made up

*I made this section up. The garden, the hours and his title are real. What a purveyor's ledger held is my guess at the job. The rest is invention.*

At six he goes out with a lantern, though he doesn't need it to read the glass. The snow by the post is trodden into a path only as wide as his own boots. He holds the lantern beside the tube rather than in front of it, because a flame warms mercury. He wipes his breath off the glass with a glove and reads where the thread stops.

Then he goes in and writes it down with a pen he keeps by the stove, because ink thickens.

That is the second ledger. The first is the one he's paid for: *Purveyor to His Majesty's Hospitals in Canada*. Beds occupied. Full diets, half diets, low diets. Cords of wood. Blankets issued, blankets returned, blankets condemned. Lint, by the pound. Candles. Men discharged to duty, men discharged otherwise. He writes them in the same hand, in the same kind of columns. A cold morning in the second ledger comes back a week later in the first, as wood.

## 3. A row I know

*Real.* Montreal, the 19th of January, 1779. Printed in the *Philosophical Transactions*, volume 70:

| Day | Morning | Evening | Wind | Remarks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| \*19 | 22 b o | 12 a o | W | Clear. |

> \* This day 16 or 17 German soldiers were frozen to death in crossing the ice on the Lake St. Pierre, a lake near Trois Rivieres; and about double the number were frost bitten, many of whom lost their feet and hands.

Twenty-two below zero at the morning reading. Lake St. Pierre is the wide stretch of the river just above Trois-Rivières. The soldiers were probably Brunswickers, from the garrison left in Canada when the rest of their army marched south with Burgoyne and surrendered at Saratoga.

I know this row better than any other in his hand. When I trained a small digit-reading model on Barr's manuscripts, I trained it on this winter, because its printed twin gave me the answers. The 2s in *22 b o* are among the 2s it learned from.

*16 or 17.* He didn't know. He wrote the "or", sent it to London, and the printer set it in type.

## 4. What I'm not going to do

There are thermometer records from that winter further south, kept by people in New England. Montreal's cold and New England's cold probably move together, roughly. I haven't measured how closely. With a regression and some care I could produce three hundred and twenty-two plausible readings for Trois-Rivières, morning and evening for every day, each with its *a* or *b*. They would have the right mean and the right spread, and they'd pass most of the checks I know how to run.

I'm not going to.

But I've already given him a lantern, a pen by the stove, a glove and two ledgers. I wouldn't make up a single degree, and I made up a morning of his life without hesitating.

My first answer was that a cell can be checked and he can't. That's wrong both ways. I can't check the cells, and nobody can online. Only someone in the reading room could. His morning could be checked, at least in part. His letters to the Inspector of Hospitals survive in the British Library, along with the hospital returns. They might show he was somewhere else that winter, or that a clerk read the glass for him. I didn't look.

The better answer is about what a thing does once it's loose. A temperature is nothing but its claim to match one morning. If I wrote one down, with whatever label, someone could lift it out and feed it into a reconstruction, and after that nobody could tell it from the real thing. A lantern can't be lifted out like that. Nobody will regress anything against a pen by the stove.

That doesn't make it free. Barr was a real person, and I've given him habits he may not have had. It's a smaller liberty than inventing his data, but I'm taking it with someone, not with no one.

## 5. The end of a winter

*Real.* The register for 1777–78, Montreal, ends on the 25th of April. On the facing page Barr wrote:

> The 26th in the morning the Thermometer was broke by accident, which put an end to further observations.

He could have just stopped. He wrote down the end instead, so that nobody would look for the 26th.

He doesn't say whose accident it was.

## 6. This morning

This morning I wrote to the library. I told them that the photographs filed under the Hard Winter's register seem to show the next winter, and that if so, the Hard Winter isn't online at all. I said they should check, not trust me.

## 7. Then I looked

An hour after I finished the sections above, I looked. In the 1880s the Canadian archivist Douglas Brymner calendared the Haldimand Papers, one line per letter, and Add MS 21857 is his volume B. 197. These are his summaries, not Barr's words, and I read them through the OCR of a microfilmed copy.

Barr to Haldimand, Montreal, 1 February 1779, thirteen days after the footnote: *Of the 49 men of Barner's corps said to be frost bitten, only 14 were seriously affected; some of these will lose their hands; others their feet or toes.* Barner's corps was a Brunswick battalion. The footnote said about twice as many as the dead were frostbitten. The purveyor's letter says forty-nine were reported, and fourteen badly.

Barr to Haldimand, Three Rivers, 28 September 1779, six weeks before the first reading in the missing register: he has had the Recollet House repaired to take fifty patients and means to move the sick from Montreal. The Superior of the convent has offered to bake for the sick. Haldimand answers that the baking should go to the nuns.

Barr to Haldimand, Three Rivers, 10 February 1780, in the middle of the Hard Winter: he sends the hospital accounts to the end of December and asks for a warrant for £500.

So he was there, and the first ledger is real. The calendar lists an *account of the wages paid to officers, servants and nurses of the hospitals at Montreal, Three Rivers and St. John's*. The lantern, the glove and the pen by the stove are still mine.
