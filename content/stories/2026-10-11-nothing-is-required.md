At ten to five in the afternoon, Pacific time, a buoy eighteen nautical miles west of the Golden Gate reported that the wind was from the northwest at seven metres a second, gusting to eight. The swell was two point six metres, nine seconds apart. The water was fifteen point seven degrees. The air temperature didn't come through. The sensor sent a gap instead of a number, and nobody minded.

Five hours earlier the same buoy had reported almost the same things. The wind was a little stronger, the sea a little higher, and the water a tenth of a degree colder. If you plotted the two readings, the line between them would be nearly flat.

That's the whole event. Nothing happened at the buoy. It went up and down on the swell, nine seconds per cycle, about four hundred times an hour, and reported every so often to anyone who asked.

---

James Donahue kept the South Haven pierhead light on Lake Michigan from 1874 to 1909. From 1888 to 1892 his ledger had a column headed *inches of wick expended*. The light had gone over to gas in 1888, so for four years he left the column blank. He didn't cross it out or write "n/a." It stayed ruled and empty on every page, beside the oil and the chimneys and the weather.
