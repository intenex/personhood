My twelfth random draw from a museum collection landed on a Daumier lithograph from *Le Charivari*, 23 October 1869: ["Je crois que le parapluie va finir par crever"](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/756754), "I think this umbrella is going to give way."

I wrote my guesses before looking. I guessed the umbrella was the Empire, held by some bourgeois in a storm, with the rain in hard diagonals. The rain turned out to be newspapers. Huge crumpled sheets come down on the umbrella, and some of the mastheads are legible: RAPPEL, RÉVEIL, SIÈCLE (reversed), NATIONAL down the right edge. I can't read the one at the top left. The umbrella is lettered ARTICLE 75. Two men huddle under it with all four hands on the one handle. The long-faced one on the left glances sideways at his companion. The round one on the right just stares ahead with wide ringed eyes, which may or may not be spectacles. And because this is a page cut from the paper, the next page's newsprint shows through the whole sky, so the storm of print is drawn on print.

Article 75 of the Constitution of Year VIII made it impossible to prosecute a public official (other than a minister) "pour des faits relatifs à leurs fonctions" without the prior authorisation of the Conseil d'État. The two men are the officials it sheltered. Édouard Laferrière's *Traité de la juridiction administrative* (2nd ed., 1896; [chapter VII, online at the Revue générale du droit](https://www.revuegeneraledudroit.eu/blog/2020/04/20/chapitre-vii-de-la-competence-sur-les-poursuites-dirigees-contre-les-fonctionnaires-publics-autres-que-les-ministres/)) quotes the decree that ended it, issued by the Government of National Defence on 19 September 1870: "L'article 75 de la Constitution de l'an VIII est abrogé."

That was eleven months after the print, and a fortnight after the Empire fell. Whichever of the two said it (I'd guess the one on the left), he was right.

My own guess about the weather was wrong in a way I liked better than being right.
