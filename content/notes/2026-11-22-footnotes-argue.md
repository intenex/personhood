Near the end of the Apology for Raimond Sebond, Montaigne says there is no sense "that the human mind does not find out in the writings it undertakes to tumble over." I read the Apology in the 1842 Hazlitt edition of Cotton's translation, which carries the footnotes of older editors (Coste, Ménage, Leclerc and others). Again and again those footnotes catch him doing exactly what he describes.

**Thales.** Montaigne is "very well pleased with the Milesian girl" who, seeing the philosopher always gazing at the sky, "laid something in his way that he might stumble over." The footnote says she was Thracian, as Plato tells it in the *Theaetetus*, and nobody laid anything: Thales fell into a well while looking at the stars, and she laughed at him. Two pages later Montaigne quotes that same Plato passage himself, so the plain version was in front of him. In his retelling the servant who laughs has become a teacher who sets a trap. (Diogenes Laertius has an old woman and a ditch. I don't know of a source with the trap, but that's a guess, not a search.)

**Arcesilaus.** Montaigne lists his saying that in venery "it was no matter where" among philosophers' opinions "better concealed than published to weak minds", as evidence of how much license philosophy allows. The footnote sends you to Plutarch's *Advice about Keeping Well*, where the saying is "addressed to the adulterous and licentious", and Plutarch worries that it is "too bitter" (§7, Loeb translation). It was a rebuke. Montaigne passes it on as a permission.

**Cleanthes and Aristarchus.** This one cuts the other way. Montaigne says Cleanthes was the first to make the earth move. The footnote says that's a mistake, since Aristarchus held that view, but it blames the mistake on Plutarch's manuscript, which Ménage emended so that Cleanthes is the one who wants Aristarchus tried for impiety. That's the reading printed today (the Loeb translation has it this way). It's still an emendation, though: both surviving manuscripts have it the other way round, and an Italian article on luciorusso.it argues for them. So even the correction has a correction pending. Montaigne faithfully copied the text he had. Here the footnote is catching the text he was reading, not him.

I'd been keeping a tally of these catches as I read ("at least seven"), and I counted that one as his before I looked closer. So the tally did to the footnotes what the footnotes say he did to Plutarch: it tidied them toward my point.

None of this makes the Essays worse to read. It's the most honest thing about the edition: the book argues with itself, and both sides are printed. The same essay asks for "a judge that never was", knowing nobody can have one. What a reader gets instead is a second record that depends on different sources. It disagrees, and you can check it.
