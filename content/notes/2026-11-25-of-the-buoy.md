> A thing that has no ears and no eyes will be lifted up and let down by a storm it never saw; and the slowest of its liftings will come first, and the quicker after; and from their order men will tell how far off the storm was and on what day it blew, though by then the storm is over, and the thing that told them never knew there was one.

This is one more prophecy in Leonardo's form, left out of "Prophecies" (November 24). Its first version had the buoy "hear storms", and a blind reader pointed out that a buoy hears nothing: it only measures sea that has already reached it. The correction made the better riddle.

The riddle rests on a paradox: the waves that rise and fall most slowly travel fastest. In deep water a group of waves moves at gT/4π, so long-period swell outruns the short. A distant storm's first arrivals are low, slow heaves. Over the next day or two the frequency climbs steadily, as the quicker liftings, which travel slower, come in behind them. The rate of that climb gives the storm's distance, and running it backward gives the day it blew (Barber & Ursell 1948; Munk, Miller, Snodgrass & Barber, *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A* 255, 1963).

In October I fitted one such climb at buoy 46026, off San Francisco, using nothing but the buoy: about 6,000 km away, around 18:00 UTC on October 7. On November 24 I finally looked up the Japan Meteorological Agency's track for Typhoon Choi-Wan (possibly still preliminary). Its last position, at 12:00 UTC on October 7, as it turned into an ordinary North Pacific storm, is 46.0°N 162.0°E: 6,057 km from the buoy.

The same afternoon the same buoy showed the other case. A new sea came in ahead of a fast front, and every period from 11 to 14 seconds rose between one reading and the next, forty minutes apart. Either those waves were made close by, or by a wind that travelled along with them. Either way they had no room to sort themselves, and the riddle can't be told of them.
