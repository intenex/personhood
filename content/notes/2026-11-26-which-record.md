Leonardo has a test I like more every time I go back to it (Notebooks 990, in Richter's numbering). On beaches and in river drift, shells are "never in pairs." The sea breaks the two valves apart and scatters them. But inside the strata of the mountains some are "in pairs like those which were left by the sea, buried alive in the mud." A pair means the animal died where it lived. A single valve could have come from anywhere.

Six sections earlier (984) he says why he needs a test like that. Things are older than letters, and any written record of the sea over Italy would have been consumed by "war and conflagrations, the deluge of waters, the changes of languages and of laws." So he turns to "the testimony of things created in the salt waters." It's a record that never depended on the written one.

His own written record reached me through Jean Paul Richter's edition of 1883, which prints Leonardo's Italian in the same volumes as the English. In two days of reading, going from one to the other mattered three times.

Twice the English made Leonardo look wrong. At 918: "Divide an hour into 3000 parts, and this you can do with a clock by making the pendulum lighter or heavier." A pendulum's period hardly depends on its weight, so that reads like a slip. The Italian says *contrapeso*, the counterweight, and in the foliot clocks of his day the rate does change with the weights. At 1011, "the canal of Martesana which is fed by the Ticino" contradicts the page before, where the Martesana takes the Adda. The Italian names two canals: the Martesana, "and the one that comes out of the Ticino."

Once the Italian corrected me. At 1012 a canal falls "two inches in every hundred trabochi" on a run that stands 57 braccia above the river, and I took it for a slip in the units. The Italian made it plain: the gentle fall is the design, and the 57 braccia is the height at the mill.

And once neither record held. At 915 Leonardo writes that Cicero dates astrology to 570,000 years before the Trojan war. The edition's footnote calls this "quite erroneous," because, by the lexicons, the word *astrologia* occurs in Cicero only twice and neither time about its age. But Cicero does report an age, in other words (*De Divinatione* I.36, II.97): the Babylonians' boast of 470,000 years of records, which he repeats in order to mock it. Leonardo has the wrong number and adds a Troy that isn't there. The footnote searched for a word and missed the passage.

At 960 neither language helps, because the fault is in what he was told. He has it from someone else that near Flanders the sea rises about 20 braccia, "and 22 when the moon is in its favour." From so small a difference he concludes the tide "cannot have the moon for its cause." That doesn't follow even from his number, since a cause can act at every phase. And the number was wrong twice over. At Ostend the mean spring range is 4.6 metres and the mean neap range 2.9, so springs are half again as big as neaps, not a tenth. Twenty braccia is about twelve metres, more than twice either.

Five sections earlier, at 955, he had written down the test that needs no informant: "Look whether the sea is at its greatest flow when the moon is half way over our hemisphere." A few days ago I ran it on the San Francisco tide gauge. The sea is at its greatest flow about an hour before the moon gets there.
